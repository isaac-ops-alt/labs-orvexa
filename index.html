AI & Automation·5 min read

How businesses can identify their first AI automation opportunity

Most businesses don't need an AI strategy document. They need one process that's currently wasting hours every week, and a clear-eyed look at whether AI can genuinely fix it.

Start with pain, not possibility

It's tempting to start by asking "where could we use AI?" That question is too open — almost anything qualifies, which means nothing gets prioritised. A better starting question is: what task does someone on your team dread doing every week because it's repetitive, manual, and doesn't require real judgement?

Look for structured, repeatable work

AI and automation are strongest where the input and output are predictable — answering the same category of question, sorting the same type of document, following the same decision tree every time. Highly judgement-based, relationship-driven work is usually the wrong place to start.

Measure before you build

Before recommending anything, we ask how often a task happens, how long it takes, and who does it. If a task takes 20 minutes a week, it's rarely worth automating yet. If it takes 20 minutes a day across three people, that's a real opportunity.

The first step is usually small

The businesses that succeed with AI don't start with a company-wide rollout. They start with one workflow, prove it works, and expand from there — which is exactly how we approach an AI workflow audit.

AI & Business·4 min read

What AI actually means for employees in 2026

Most of the anxiety around AI in the workplace comes from vague headlines, not lived experience. The teams we work with tend to feel very differently about AI once they've actually used it on a real task.

It's a shift in what fills your day, not whether you have one

For most roles, AI is removing the repetitive 20% of the job — drafting the first version of something, summarising a document, sorting through routine requests. What's left is usually the part people found more interesting to begin with.

The skill that matters most is knowing when to trust it

AI tools are confident even when they're wrong. The employees who get the most value from AI aren't the ones who use it for everything — they're the ones who've learned where it's reliable and where it needs a second look. That's a skill, and it's teachable.

Education beats mandate

Rolling out an AI tool without explaining how it works, what it's good at, and what it isn't, tends to create either blind over-reliance or total avoidance. A short, practical session usually does more than a company-wide policy document.

Automation·6 min read

5 business workflows that are ready for automation

Some workflows are automation-ready right now — the pattern is predictable, the volume is high enough to matter, and the current process is entirely manual. Here are five we see most often.

1. Lead follow-up and qualification

New enquiries that sit unanswered for a day lose momentum fast. Automated first-response and qualification means every lead gets touched immediately, with a human stepping in once it's worth their time.

2. Invoice and payment chasing

Chasing overdue payments is repetitive, slightly awkward, and easy to automate with clear rules and escalation steps — without losing the option for a human to step in on sensitive accounts.

3. Customer support triage

Most support tickets fall into a handful of categories. Automatically routing, tagging and drafting first-response answers frees your team to focus on the tickets that actually need judgement.

4. Internal reporting

If someone on your team spends hours each week copying numbers between systems to build a report, that's almost always automatable — and it's one of the fastest wins we implement.

5. Onboarding new clients or staff

Document collection, account setup and welcome sequences follow the same steps every time. Automating the repetitive parts lets the human touchpoints feel more intentional, not less.

AI & Product·7 min read

Building AI products responsibly

It's easy to bolt an AI feature onto a product and call it innovation. It's harder — and more important — to think carefully about what that feature actually does to the person using it.

Be honest about what the product is, and isn't

This is something we think about directly with OverCome, our own AI-powered behaviour-change companion. It's designed to help people understand their patterns and get through difficult moments — it is not, and doesn't claim to be, a medical or therapeutic tool. Being precise about that distinction matters more than sounding impressive.

Design for the moment that matters

A lot of behaviour-change products default to gamification — streaks, badges, counters. We've chosen a different starting point for OverCome's MVP: the immediate craving. Streaks are easy to build and just as easy to break, and breaking one often pushes someone further into the behaviour they're trying to change. Support that shows up in the actual difficult moment tends to matter more than a number going up.

Privacy has to be architecture, not a policy page

When a product deals with something personal, privacy-conscious design needs to be a decision made at the data-model level, not a promise made in a privacy policy afterward.

Ship less, and be clear about what's next

Calling something "in development" instead of overselling it as finished isn't a weakness — it's what lets you build the next version based on what's actually true, not what sounded good in the pitch.

Cybersecurity·5 min read

Why cybersecurity needs to evolve with AI

AI changes two things at once: what's worth protecting, and how the people trying to get past your defences operate. Security thinking has to account for both.

New surface area

Once a business connects an AI assistant to internal data, or lets a model take actions on its behalf, that integration becomes part of the attack surface. Access controls, data boundaries and audit trails need to be designed in from the start — not added once something goes wrong.

Attacks are getting more convincing

Phishing emails, fake support messages and impersonation attempts are harder to spot when they're written well and personalised at scale. Technical defences matter, but so does re-training people's instincts for what to trust.

Security-conscious engineering is a default, not an add-on

We treat this as a baseline requirement for anything we build, whether it's a client system or one of our own products — data handled with intention, access scoped tightly, and privacy considered at the architecture stage rather than bolted on later.

Product Development·5 min read

From idea to MVP: how technology products should actually be built

Most early-stage products fail not because the idea was bad, but because the MVP tried to do too much. This is the thinking we apply to our own product, OverCome, and to any product work we take on.

Find the one moment that matters most

Every product idea has dozens of features that feel necessary. Almost none of them are, at first. The job at MVP stage is finding the single moment where your product needs to prove its value — for OverCome, that's the immediate craving, not the long-term habit tracker.

Design around that moment, not around the feature list

Once you know the moment that matters, most feature decisions become easier: does this help someone in that exact moment, or is it something that can wait for version two?

Build it like it has to work, not like it has to impress

An MVP's job is to be honest with you about whether the idea works. Overbuilding the interface before validating the core mechanic just delays finding out.

Treat "in development" as an honest, useful state

A product doesn't need to be finished to be real. Being upfront that something is still in development — while showing the thinking behind it — builds more trust than pretending it's more finished than it is.
